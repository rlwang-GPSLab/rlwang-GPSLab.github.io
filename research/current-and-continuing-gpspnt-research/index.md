  <h2 class="research-subheading">WAAS/SBAS</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/waas-square-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="WAAS/SBAS thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Satellite Based Augmentation Systems (SBAS) augment GNSS, enabling planes to
    make precision approaches and landings. The Wide Area Augmentation System
    (WAAS) is the U.S. implementation of SBAS. SBAS combines core constellation
    satellites with geostationary satellites and ground-based reference stations to
    monitor satellite ranging errors. Satellite ranging errors include: ephemeris,
    clock, ionosphere, troposphere, multipath &amp; receiver noise.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/waassbas">More information about WAAS/SBAS</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">LAAS/GBAS</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/laas-gbas-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="LAAS/GBAS thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    The Local Area Augmentation System (LAAS), now more commonly known as the
    Ground Based Augmentation System (GBAS), is an all-weather aircraft landing
    system based on real-time differential correction of the
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System" target="_blank" rel="noopener">GPS</a>
    signal. Local reference
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Receiver_(radio)" target="_blank" rel="noopener">receivers</a>
    located around the airport send measurements to a nearby processing unit, which
    use these measurements to formulate differential corrections for the GPS
    satellites being tracked by the reference receivers.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/laasgbas">More about LAAS / GBAS</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Cyber Safety for Transportation</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/cyber-safety-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Cyber Safety for Transportation thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Tomorrow's transportation systems will be much more automated. It's likely to
    include: automatic vehicles that will descend from the air and populate our
    roadways (automatic driving assistance, or ADAS), railways (positive train
    control, PTC) and waterways (ships without crews). Moreover, the sky will be
    filled with aircraft that carry no pilots to mitigate flight risk. These will
    be drones or un-piloted air vehicles (UAVs). For efficiency, cars will drive
    while the enclosed humans snooze or send texts. Trains will slow or speed
    certain that they alone occupy the underlying track. Drones will fly
    confidently between buildings to monitor air pollution and crime in our major
    cities.
  </p>
  <p>
    Securing tomorrow's transportation system will involve many challenges. To
    address these challenges, SCPNT is sponsoring a number of research projects
    including Jamming Resistance, Anti-Spoofing, JAGER and Alternative PNT.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/cyber-safety-transportation">More about Cyber Safety for Transportation</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Time &amp; Time Transfer</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/time-transfer-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Time and Time Transfer diagram"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Currently, microwave time transfer systems between ground and space reach a
    level of precision on the order of the nanosecond (about 1 to 30 ns achieved
    with GPS). An integration over ~30 days would be necessary to reach the
    10<sup>−15</sup> level. That cannot support the performance requirements and
    goals of future optical atomic clock in space missions (such as ESA-SOC), which
    aim for the 10<sup>−17</sup> level or better.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/time-time-transfer">More information about Time and Time Transfer</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">IMU Development &amp; Testing</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/imu-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="IMU Development & Testing thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    An inertial measurement unit (IMU) is an electronic device that measures and
    reports a body's
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_force" target="_blank" rel="noopener">specific force</a>,
    angular rate, and sometimes the magnetic field surrounding the body, using a
    combination of accelerometers and gyroscopes, and sometimes magnetometers. IMUs
    are typically used to maneuver
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aircraft" target="_blank" rel="noopener">aircraft</a>,
    including UAVs, and spacecraft, including satellites and landers. Recent
    developments allow for the production of IMU-enabled GPS devices. An IMU allows
    a GPS receiver to work when GPS signals are unavailable, such as in tunnels,
    inside buildings, or when electronic interference is present.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/imu-development-testing">More information about IMU Development and Testing</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Multi-Constellation GNSS</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/multi-constellation-gnss-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Multi-Constellation GNSS thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Over the lifetime of SCPNT, the world of global positioning and satellite-based
    global navigation has been evolving dramatically. The U.S. Global Positioning
    System (GPS) and the Russian GLONASS satellite system have been joined by the
    Chinese BeiDou system, the European Union's Galileo system, Japan's
    Quasi-Zenith Satellite System (QZSS), the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite
    System (IRNSS) and others. Stanford is at the forefront of using interoperable
    signals from these satellite constellations to perform navigational tasks in
    various environments.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/multi-constellation-gnss">More about multi-constellation GNSS</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Marine Animals Tracking</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/marine-animals-tracking-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Marine Animals Tracking thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Stanford's Hopkins Marine Station, led by Professor Barbara Block, has been
    pioneering the use of GPS tags to study the migratory behavior of large marine
    animals—especially tuna, sharks, whales and turtles. The GPS Lab and SCPNT have
    recently teamed with Hopkins Marine to develop a GPS tag that can provide a
    marine animal's GPS position location in near real-time. The tag, when
    activated, transmits the animal's position using
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_phone" target="_blank" rel="noopener">satellite phone (satphone)</a>
    technology. The tag is being developed to detect illegal shark fishing. It could
    also have other similar applications.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/marine-animals-tracking">More information about Marine Animals Tracking</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Positive Train Control</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/positive-train-control-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Positive Train Control thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Positive train control (PTC) is a system of functional requirements for
    monitoring and controlling train movements, as an attempt to provide increased
    safety. GPS and other GNSS systems may be deployed as a reliable and continuous
    positioning system, suitable for train control. Stanford has been performing
    research in this area, with techniques similar to those used with WAAS
    certification. These research techniques include the development of software
    code to model rail safety issues caused by multipath and the ionosphere.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/positive-train-control">More information about Positive Train Control (PTC)</a>
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">GNSS Software Receiver</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/gnss-software-receiver-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="GNSS Software Receiver thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    A GNSS software receiver is an implementation that has been designed and built
    following the philosophy of
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software-defined_radio" target="_blank" rel="noopener">software-defined radio</a>.
    This is done using a reconfigurable computational platform such as a
    microprocessor, digital signal processing element, graphics processor, or
    field-programmable gate array. This is in contrast with a traditional GNSS
    receiver implementation, which leverages a hardwired application-specific
    integrated circuit (ASIC). The software receiver provides maximum flexibility
    and the ability to redesign the architecture quickly and efficiently, allowing
    candidate signal-processing algorithms to be designed and assessed.
  </p>
  <p>
    <a href="/research/current-and-continuing-gpspnt-research/gnss-software-receiver">More information about GNSS Software Receivers</a>
  </p>
