  <h1>Multi-Constellation GNSS</h1>

  <p class="research-intro">
    Over the past 15 years, the world of global positioning and satellite-based
    global navigation has been evolving dramatically. The U.S. Global Positioning
    System (GPS) and the Russian GLONASS satellite system have been joined by the
    Chinese BeiDou system, the European Union's Galileo system, Japan's
    Quasi-Zenith Satellite System (QZSS), the Indian Regional Navigation Satellite
    System (IRNSS) and others. Stanford is at the forefront of using interoperable
    signals from these satellite constellations to perform navigational tasks in
    various environments.
  </p>

  <p>
    The two projects below—Advanced RAIM and Navigation via Commercial LEO
    Satellites—are examples of Stanford research in this area.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Advanced RAIM</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/araim-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Advanced RAIM photo thumbnail"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    ARAIM (Advanced RAIM), employed in some receivers, provides fault detection
    and exclusion (FDE) by using a minimum of six satellites, not only to detect a
    possibly faulty satellite, but also to exclude that satellite from the
    navigation solution so that the navigation function can continue without
    interruption. The goal of ARAIM is to detect the presence of a positioning
    failure. Upon detection, proper fault exclusion determines and excludes the
    source of the failure (without necessarily identifying the individual source
    causing the problem), thereby allowing
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener">GNSS</a>
    navigation to continue without interruption. The use of satellites from
    multiple GNSS constellations or the use of SBAS satellites as additional
    ranging sources can improve the availability of RAIM and FDE.
  </p>

  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Navigation Via Commercial LEO Satellites</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/leo-nav-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Navigation Via Commercial LEO Satellites graphic thumbnail"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    The Global Positioning System (GPS) is a vital component of systems ranging
    from the power grid to wireless communications and even landing aircraft.
    However, GPS does have its drawbacks: it is easy to jam and it is very
    expensive to both operate and maintain. The challenge is to improve the
    resilience of GPS while decreasing the cost of this now-essential
    infrastructure.
  </p>
  <p>
    To solve this, we look towards the changing market of space. New players are
    coming with proposals to build constellations of hundreds and even thousands
    of satellites in low Earth orbit (LEO). Their aim is delivering Internet to
    the world by providing global broadband coverage. We focus on how such
    constellations could be leveraged to carry a hosted payload, allowing them to
    act as navigation satellites.
  </p>
