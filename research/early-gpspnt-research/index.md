  <h1>Early GPS/PNT Research</h1>

  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/early-gps-collage-banner.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Early GPS research photo collage banner image"
       class="research-page-image">

  <p class="research-intro">
    The 1990's and early 2000's were an extremely productive period for
    innovative PNT research at Stanford. Stanford's landmark Gravity Probe B
    Mission (GP-B), which ran from 1963–2011, pioneered the use of GPS technology
    for precision spacecraft attitude and translation control. Many spinoff uses
    of GPS technology emerged from GP-B, including:
  </p>
  <ul class="research-link-list">
    <li>Autonomous Aircraft Control</li>
    <li>Airplane Navigation—Takeoffs and Landings</li>
    <li>Precision Farming/Agriculture</li>
    <li>Enhanced guidance &amp; situational awareness for aircraft</li>
  </ul>

  <p>
    View a paper showing Stanford doctoral student leadership in GPS research:
    <a href="http://web.stanford.edu/group/scpnt/jse_website/documents/Mallette-GPS_Doctoral_Publishing_Trends-ION-2006.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
      Professional Publishing Trends of Recent GPS Doctoral Students
    </a>, by Leo Mallette, ION Conference Presentation, 2006.
  </p>

  <p>
    Below is a list of early Stanford GPS Lab research projects. Click the "More
    Information" link or the corresponding sub-menu item to the left to view
    details about a project.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">GPS for Spacecraft Attitude &amp; Translation Control</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/gpb-spacecraft-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="GPS for Spacecraft Attitude & Translation Control thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    A commercial GPS receiver was used for the first time to help determine the
    attitude (i.e. orientation or flight dynamics) of a spacecraft on the
    Stanford-led Gravity Probe B (GP-B) Mission. In the late 1980s, a special GPS
    receiver was developed that could accommodate a rolling spacecraft (i.e. about
    1 rpm) in low Earth orbit. The spacecraft had extreme pointing requirements
    and needed to maintain its orbit to within +/- 25 meters. The spacecraft's
    attitude control system performed flawlessly on-orbit during the 18-month
    mission.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Autonomous Aircraft</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/autonomous-aircraft-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Autonomous Aircraft thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Stanford demonstrated the first non-military GPS-guided autonomous aircraft
    (or <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unmanned_aerial_vehicle" target="_blank" rel="noopener">UAV</a>)
    circa 1996. Commercial, non-military GPS-guided UAVs (or drones) have become
    commonplace for use by commercial companies and/or hobbyists.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">GPS for Airplane Navigation, Takeoffs and Landings</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/airplane-navigation-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="GPS for Airplane Navigation, Takeoffs and Landings thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    A commercial GPS receiver and multiple antennas were mounted on a small Cessna
    airplane to perform local flight tests near Stanford in the early 1990's. The
    tests showed that GPS could provide aviation attitude control for en route and
    precision approaches at the Palo Alto airport. The tests were broadened to
    include local terrestrial
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudolite" target="_blank" rel="noopener">pseudolites</a>
    transmitting a GPS signal, which allowed for successful automatic landing of
    the Cessna.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Precision Farming/Agriculture</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/gps-tractor-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Precision Farming/Agriculture thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Stanford first demonstrated precision agriculture in the mid 1990's. The
    practice of precision agriculture was enabled by the advent of GPS and GNSS
    ability to locate precise position in a field for the creation of maps of the
    spatial variability of as many variables as can be measured. The practice of
    precision agriculture allows the farmer to proactively manage crop performance
    in several ways, including: crop yield, terrain features/topography, organic
    matter content, moisture levels, nitrogen levels, pH, EC, Mg, K, etc.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">RAIM</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/raim-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="RAIM thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Receiver autonomous integrity monitoring (RAIM) is a technology developed to
    assess the integrity of GPS signals in a GPS receiver system. It is of special
    importance in safety-critical GPS applications, such as in aviation or marine
    navigation. RAIM detects faults with redundant GPS pseudorange measurements.
    That is, when more satellites are available than needed to produce a position
    fix, the extra pseudoranges should all be consistent with the computed
    position. A pseudorange that differs significantly from the expected value
    (i.e., an outlier) may indicate a fault of the associated satellite or another
    signal integrity problem (e.g., ionospheric dispersion).
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">JPALS</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/jpals-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="JPALS thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    The Joint Precision Approach and Landing System (JPALS) is a military,
    all-weather landing system based on real-time, dual-frequency (L1 and L2)
    local-area differential corrections for
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Positioning_System" target="_blank" rel="noopener">GPS</a>
    signals, augmented with integrity information, and transmitted to the user via
    secure means. JPALS development started from the civilian LAAS/GBAS system
    (see above) and split into two separate systems. One of these is known as
    Local Differential GPS (LDGPS), which is used for airfields on land and
    includes a variant that can be set up quickly.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">eLORAN</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/eloran-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="eLORAN thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    eLoran is a low-frequency terrestrial navigation system based on a number of
    transmission stations, which emit precisely timed and shaped radio pulses
    centred at 100 kHz radio frequency. LORAN, short for LOng-RAnge Navigation,
    was a
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_navigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener">hyperbolic</a>
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_navigation" target="_blank" rel="noopener">radio navigation</a>
    system developed in the United States during World War II. It was first used
    for ship convoys crossing the Atlantic Ocean, and then by long-range patrol
    aircraft. The US Navy began development of Loran-B, which offered accuracy on
    the order of a few tens of feet, but ran into significant technical problems.
    The US Air Force had worked on a different concept, which the Navy picked up as
    <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loran-C" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Loran-C</a>.
    Loran-C offered longer range than LORAN and accuracy of hundreds of feet. The
    US Coast Guard took over operations of both systems in 1958.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">GPS Tunnel in the Sky</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/gps-tunnel-in-the-sky-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="GPS Tunnel in the Sky thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    Three-dimensional flight displays can increase the efficiency of remote
    sensing flight operations by providing enhanced guidance and situational
    awareness on straight and curved flight paths. Such a display depicts an
    "out-the-window" perspective view of the world along with a tunnel through
    which the pilot flies the aircraft. Using differential GPS, inexpensive
    graphics hardware, and a flat-panel display, a prototype system was built and
    flight tested to demonstrate significant advantages over conventional
    instruments.
  </p>

  <h2 class="research-subheading">Arctic Navigation and ION GNSS</h2>
  <img src="{{ '/assets/img/research/arctic-navigation-thumb.jpg' | relative_url }}"
       alt="Arctic Navigation and ION GNSS thumbnail image"
       class="research-content-image">
  <p>
    The once inaccessible Arctic Ocean has gained economic attention as a result
    of the recession of the Arctic sea ice. This has triggered the expansion of
    many industries in the Arctic, some prospective and others very real and
    rapidly expanding. This growing activity, along with the harsh environment and
    remote reaches of the Arctic, necessitates the highest levels of safety, using
    a multi-tiered approach.
  </p>
